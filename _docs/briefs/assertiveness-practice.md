# Saying it out loud

The practice half of assertiveness. Not the beliefs -- those are already
written. This is the sentence you say, out loud, to a person who is pushing
back.

Built: the first lesson, "say it with I", at `/practice/say-i`. Drills A
and B and the seventeen remaining sentences are written here and not built.

| --- | --- |

## Why this exists

The 44 lines all answer a belief. Sheet I of the handout is the same shape:
nineteen rows, a rule swapped for a right.

**A right removes the reason not to. It does not supply the words.** Somebody
can agree they are allowed to say no and still have no sentence ready when it
is their turn to speak.

Sheet II supplies the sentence. Reading it does nothing either -- a technique
is a thing you say, in a voice, at normal volume, while somebody pushes back.

So the unit here is not a line you read. It is a line you say.

## What the reader never sees

The handout names its techniques: basic assertion, empathic assertion, broken
record. **None of those words reach the reader**, under rule 12. They are how
this file talks, not how the app does.

## One rule is bent on purpose

Rule 2 bans instructions -- "should", "try to", "remember to" -- because they
pile into a to-do list nobody asked for.

**"Say it out loud" is an instruction and it stays.** The difference is that
the reader opened a practice screen. An instruction inside a thing somebody
chose to do is the thing they chose. An instruction arriving on a lock screen
is not.

It is said once, plainly, and never repeated on the same screen.

## Spoken lines take contractions

The affirmation lines avoid them -- a line read at a glance is cleaner
without. These are read out loud, and "I would rather not" is not a sentence
anybody says.

Every spoken line here contracts.

# The rule under everything: say "I"

Sheet II gives it straight: basic assertion uses "I" statements. It is the
most portable thing in the handout and it decides whether a sentence lands or
starts a row.

**A sentence about the other person puts them on the back foot.** They stop
hearing what happened and start defending who they are. Nothing after that
gets in.

| Points at | Example | Use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who they are | "You're so thoughtless." | Never |
| What they did | "When you leave it this late..." | Yes |
| You | "I didn't know about that." | Yes |
| The thing that happened | "That hurt." | Yes |

**The rule is not "never say you".** The handout's own example says "you" and
is right: *"When you leave it this late to produce your report..."* The word
is fine on a behaviour. It is the word on a character that starts the row.

Its step 1 says why in its own terms: describe the behaviour **without
interpreting or judging**. "Late" is a fact. "Thoughtless" is a verdict, and a
verdict is a thing to argue with.

Three words give it away nearly every time:

- **always** -- "You always leave it late."
- **never** -- "You never tell me."
- **so** -- "You're so difficult."

## This is not the app's rule reversed

Easy to misread later, so it is written down.

| Who is speaking | Which word | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The app, to the reader | "you" | Rule 1. Distance lowers how hard a feeling lands |
| The reader, to another person | "I" | Sheet II. It keeps the other person off the defensive |

Two speakers, two jobs. Neither cancels the other.

# The screen

Settled 20 September 2026, after an illustrated version was tried and
dropped.

## Illustrating the scenario does not work

Nineteen rights means nineteen-plus scenarios. Drawing each one means deciding
which feeling fits, nineteen times, and each decision can be wrong.

It is also the wrong target. **The scenario changes every time. The mechanism
never does.**

Two things are true in every drill:

1. Their words keep changing.
2. Your line does not.

## So the layout is the lesson

**Still true, and the arrangement below was superseded on 20 September 2026 --
see "Context comes before practice".** The rule survives; the stepped screen
it was first drawn for did not.

```

  │                               │
  │   "It's only two minutes."    │  theirs
  │                               │  new every time
  │                               │  arrives, then goes
  │   ─────────────────────────   │

  │                               │
```

Their line arrives and goes. Yours sits still, in the same box, at the same
size, from the first push to the last.

**The app already holds this rule.** On the breathing screen the sidekick must
never shift, because a thing that moves while you are matching it has moved on
you. Your sentence is the fixed thing here, for the same reason.

| | Per-scenario art | Your line not moving |
| --- | --- | --- |

## What the sidekick does

Nothing. One idle pose, below the lines.

She is not teaching and she is not the person pushing back. She is company,
which is what fixes the embarrassment. An illustration does not.

## Cloud, fire and wave are not cancelled

Shapes standing for a feeling were the right instinct in the wrong drill.
Here, the feeling is not the topic -- the sentence is.

They belong to the self-compassion lessons, where the feeling **is** the
topic. Kept for that, out of this.

## Rule 4 and pictures

Worth writing down, because somebody will raise it.

Rule 4 bans metaphors. A fire standing for heat is one.

It survives, narrowly, and only as a picture. Rule 4 exists because a
comparison in a *sentence* makes the reader hold two things and work out which
part was meant. That is a puzzle. A picture is read before words happen, so
there is nothing to work out.

**Show it, never say it.** The ban holds on every caption, every time.

## Context comes before practice, and the Next button is gone

Settled 20 September 2026, after the stepped version was built and looked at.

The first build handed the reader one sentence at a time behind a Next
button, and opened on the fourth line of the argument. Three faults, and they
are the same fault:

| What the reader got | What was missing |
| --- | --- |
| "Say \"you\", and they stop hearing what happened." | Any reason to be on this screen |
| A line, a button, a line, a button | Any idea how long it went on for |
| Two lines at a time | The shape -- theirs changing, yours not |

**The layout was the lesson and one sentence at a time could not show it.**
The whole point is that the "you" line keeps changing and the "I" line does
not. Two lines on a screen is the one arrangement where that is invisible.

So the page is read top to bottom, in groups:

```
  ┌───────────────────────────────┐
  │            (her)              │  fixed, one idle pose
  │ ───────────────────────────── │
  │  Saying it out loud           │  what this is
  │  three short lines            │
  │                               │
  │  Today: saying it with "I"    │  the rule, and why
  │  two short paragraphs         │
  │                               │
  │  Pick one. Say the second     │  the one instruction
  │  line out loud.               │
  │                               │
  │  ▸ They let you down.         │  two situations
  │  ▸ Something wasn't fair.     │  one opens at a time
  │                               │
  │  The one that catches         │  the closer
  │  everybody                    │
  └───────────────────────────────┘
```

**Superseded again on 20 September 2026: the page is five chapters.**
`_docs/briefs/practice-tab-layout.md` holds the argument. The grouping above
survives untouched -- what went is the single scroll that held all five groups
at once, which is a wall on a phone. A forward control comes back, and the
piece behind it is a whole group rather than one sentence. **"Your turn" is one
chapter and may never be cut in two**, which is this section's rule restated.

**Two situations, two swaps inside each.** Four loose sentences in a row read
as a list to get through. A situation the reader picks is a thing that
happened to them, and the two lines under it are the same sentence said about
two different days. Open one and the other closes.

**Nothing is behind a press except the lines.** The reader scrolls; they do
not advance.

## The house voice is not this screen's voice

Settled 20 September 2026. The first build followed
`_docs/kind-writing-style.md` to the letter, and the screen came out with no
name for its own subject.

**That file was written for a different job.** It is the app talking to
somebody on a hard evening: a line on a lock screen, an empty state, an error
message, the account offer. Somebody in that position did not ask to be
taught, so a technique name is jargon, an instruction is a chore, and "we" is
a brochure claiming a togetherness the app has not earned.

**Somebody who opened a practice screen asked to be taught.** Everything
changes with that. A lesson that will not name its own subject teaches a trick
rather than a skill, and leaves the reader with nothing to look up, nothing to
recognise next time, and no idea what they just did.

So the rules split in two.

| Rule | On a lock screen | In a lesson |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 -- never "we" | Holds. It claims a closeness the app has not got | Dropped. A teacher says "today we're practising" |
| 2 -- no instructions | Holds. They pile into a to-do list nobody asked for | Dropped. The instruction *is* the lesson |
| 12 -- no naming a technique | Holds. Jargon in front of somebody struggling | Dropped. The name is the thing being taught |
| 9 -- no promise | Holds, **and holds here**, hedged: "it can ease anxiety", never "it will" |
| 7 -- no claim about the reader | Holds, **and holds here**. The feeling is offered, never assumed |
| 15 -- nothing counts, always a way out | Holds, **and holds here**. No score, no tally, "That's enough for now" |

**The line between them is who asked.** A rule that protects somebody who did
not ask for this is kept. A rule that stops a teacher teaching is dropped.

`_docs/kind-writing-style.md` names the practice screens in its own scope
line, so this does not have to be rediscovered.

## Four parts, not two. The handout was read properly on 20 September 2026

The build before this taught **what happened** and **what you'd like**, with
the feeling offered as an optional extra. That is two of the handout's four
steps, and the missing one is the one that does the work.

Its **negative feelings assertion** is four steps, in order:

| Step | Its own wording | Its example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Describe the other person's behaviour objectively. Without interpreting or judging | *"When you leave it this late to produce your report..."* |
| 2 | Describe the impact on you. Be specific, don't overgeneralise | *"...it involves my working over the weekend..."* |
| 3 | Describe your feelings | *"...I feel annoyed about this..."* |
| 4 | State how you'd prefer it in future | *"...so in future I'd like to receive it by Friday lunch time"* |

**Step 2 was the one missing, and it is the one that makes a request land.**
"I've been sitting here an hour" is a fact nobody has to care about. "I've been
sitting here an hour and I've had to cancel my evening" is the same fact with a
cost attached. A request with no cost behind it is a preference.

Every scenario line was rewritten to carry a cost.

**Four steps are taught as optional on purpose.** Four parts read as a form to
fill in, and somebody who cannot manage all four says nothing at all. The
screen says so in the handout's own terms: two is a finished sentence, four is
the strongest one. Its other warning goes next to it -- *"Avoid unnecessary
padding and keep your statement simple and brief."*

## How a skill is actually taught, and what was missing

Skills training runs **instruction, modelling, rehearsal, feedback**. The build
before this did the first two and stopped.

| Stage | On the screen |
| --- | --- |
| Instruction | "Assertiveness" and "Today: the \"I\" sentence" |
| Modelling | "The shape of it" -- the four parts on one worked sentence |
| Rehearsal | "Your turn" -- the two situations, said out loud |
| Feedback | The app cannot hear anyone, so the handout's self-review stands in |

**The last section is the one a lesson usually drops, and it is three
paragraphs of the handout nearly word for word:**

- *"begin practising them in a neutral situation... where your emotions aren't
  too strong."*
- *"keep your voice calm, the volume normal, the pace even, keep good eye
  contact, and try and keep your physical tension low."*
- *"the first time you try these techniques they may not go the way you
  planned. It is important you don't beat yourself up about this but look at
  what went wrong and how you might do it differently next time. And then have
  another go! Over time you will find that they get easier."*

Without them, somebody practises on the argument they have been dreading, in a
shaky voice, and concludes they are bad at this. The words were taught and
nothing else.

**"It does get easier" is the one promise on the screen and it is the
handout's.** It is a claim about repetition, not about any conversation going
well. The price section of the handout is still to be built and still says
there is no guarantee of outcome.

## The voice is the handout's, not the app's

The user's note on the first rewrite: *"sounds not bad but can be more
conversational and natural."* Correct, and the reason is that the house voice
was still leaking through -- clipped declaratives, every sentence the same
length, no contractions where a person would use one.

The register to copy is the module's own. It writes *"By this we mean one where
your emotions aren't too strong"*, *"And then have another go!"*, *"Children
are experts at the broken record technique."* It is a person explaining
something, with asides.

So the screen contracts, uses asides, varies sentence length, and says the
awkward part out loud: *"It feels silly the first time. Everybody finds that."*

## What the page says now

> **Assertiveness**
>
> Assertiveness is asking for what you want without starting a row, and without
> going quiet either.
> It's a skill rather than a personality. Most people were never taught it, and
> it gets easier once you have the words.
> One thing worth knowing: saying how you feel out loud takes the edge off the
> anxiety. It's holding it in that keeps it going round.

> **Today: the "I" sentence**
>
> Here's the trouble with "you". Say "You always..." and the other person stops
> hearing what happened. They start defending who they are, and after that
> nothing you say gets through.
> So we turn it around. Instead of a verdict about them, you say what happened
> and what you'd like. There's nothing in that to argue with.
> It's the same thing you wanted. It's just about you now, instead of about
> them.

> **The shape of it**
>
> | Part | What it is for | Example |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | When you... | What they did. Just the facts, no verdict | *When you come home late without telling me,* |
> | it means... | What it cost you. This is the part people leave out | *I end up worrying something's happened,* |
> | I feel... | How it left you. One word is plenty | *and it makes me angry.* |
> | I'd like... | What you actually want. Without this, it's just a complaint | *I'd really like a quick ring to say you're alright.* |
>
> The examples read straight down as one sentence.

> **Your turn**
>
> Pick whichever one sounds like your week. Say the second line out loud --
> actually out loud, not in your head. It feels silly the first time. Everybody
> finds that.

> **Before you try it on somebody**
>
> Start somewhere easy...
> Watch your voice as much as your words...
> The first go probably won't come out how you planned it...

Tests pin the structure rather than the wording: four frame steps in order, the
worked example reading as one sentence, "don't need all four" present, and the
practice section mentioning easy, voice and another go.

# Drill 0 -- swap the sentence

**The first lesson. Build this one.**

Every line in every other drill is an "I" sentence. Somebody who has not got
this yet will say the right words in the wrong shape and get a row instead of
an answer.

It is also the fastest win in the set. It changes nothing about what you want.
It changes only who the sentence is about.

## The introduction

**These are the words. Settled 21 September 2026.** Four paragraphs, before the
first sentence is shown.

> This lesson is about talking to the people closest to us: partners, family,
> friends.
>
> When something's bothering us, it's easy to start with "you": "You always...",
> "You never..." But "you" statements can sound like blame. The other person
> feels attacked, gets defensive, and stops listening.
>
> "I" statements say how you feel and what you prefer instead, which makes it
> easier for the other person to hear you.
>
> Next you'll see six sentences. For each one, decide: is it a criticism, or is
> it expressing yourself?

Each paragraph does one job, and the one it replaced did none of them:

| Paragraph | Job | What the reader could not do without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Names who is on the other end | Picture the conversation. A drill about "the other person" could be about a call centre |
| 2 | Names what goes wrong, as a chain | See why it fails. Attacked, then defensive, then not listening -- three steps, not an opinion |
| 3 | Names what to do instead | Know what is being taught. The old lead said "one kind" and "the other kind" and never said what either kind was |
| 4 | Sets the question | Answer it. The drill now asks in exactly these words |

**Paragraph 4 is the one that binds the screen.** The two option cards read
**"A criticism"** and **"Expressing myself"**, and the helper under every
sentence reads **"Is that a criticism, or is it expressing yourself?"** They
were worded three different ways before this. One question asked one way is the
same rule that governs the breathing cues: a reader following one instruction
must not be handed two near-copies of it. Rewording any of the three means
rewording all four.

**"Us" and "our" are allowed here and are not a slip.** The no-"we" rule is
about the app claiming closeness it has not earned -- a lock screen line, an
empty state. This is a lesson the reader opened, and a teacher's "us" is how
the subject gets introduced. See the scope table in `CLAUDE.md`.

**Naming "I statements" is allowed here for the same reason.** The
no-naming-a-technique rule is about somebody who did not ask. This reader is
here to learn the thing, and a name is what they recognise the next time they
meet it.

### The tension in paragraph 2, on purpose

It sorts by the **pronoun**. The drill then teaches that the pronoun is not the
test:

| Card | Says | Is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | "I feel like you're being selfish." | Opens with "I". Still a criticism |
| 6 | "I'd rather you asked me first." | Says "you". Not a criticism |

That is deliberate and it is how every handout does it. The simple rule gets
somebody moving; the cards correct it while they are paying attention, which is
worth more than a hedge nobody reads. See "The one that catches everybody" and
"This is not the app's rule reversed".

**It only breaks if the drill is shortened and those two cards are the ones
cut.** They are the correction. Six sentences without them teaches the pronoun
rule and nothing else.

## The screen it is built on

**Built 21 September 2026.** `lib/features/practice/views/swap_drill_view.dart`,
on its own route `/practice/swap`, with its own row in the practice list.

**It was a second screen rather than a sixth chapter of the reading lesson,
and on 21 September 2026 it became the only screen.** The lesson was a scroll
and this is a stepper -- two shapes wearing one name, teaching one subject,
on one tab. The stepper is the one that survived, because the point here is

| Part | What it is |
| --- | --- |

**One bar, and no segments.** An earlier prototype ran two -- one for the six

thirteen ticks in a row is "3 of 13" drawn instead of written, and rule 15
bans the number either way. A test asserts progress only ever rises.

**No arrow beside the label.** The label says what pressing it does. A test
fails any forward label carrying one.

**The ground is a fixed off-white, `#F4F3EF`, in every theme.**

- An exercise should read the same on the sixth visit as on the first. A

- A lesson is long-form reading, and off-white is the quietest ground text can
  sit on -- no hue to tint the ink, a fraction under white so it does not
  glare.

It is the same call the tighten screen made, the other way round: there the
orb is fixed and the page is the palette. Green and red are fixed too, because
they carry meaning and a theme that can recolour a wrong answer can lie about
it. The **accent** still paints the bar and the pill: two small marks of the
user's own palette on an otherwise neutral page.

`test/exercise_contrast_test.dart` asserts every pair clears 4.5:1. It carries
no audit baseline, unlike `contrast_test.dart` -- this ground is new, so there
is nothing to grandfather.

### The character says the sentences

**She holds each of the six in a speech bubble, and that is why she is on this
screen at all.** The plan was the opposite: an earlier build note had her
*removed* from the lesson for idling, which is the rule that took her off the
tighten screen -- a character with no job is Rive work for nothing. A sentence
in a bubble is a job.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Where | Top of the step, pinned to the top of a fixed 88×104 band |
| What moves | The bubble, downward. **She never does** |
| Which steps | The six sentences only |
| Taps | **Off.** `IgnorePointer` |

**The quote marks came off with the heading.** The sentence used to be a big
bold heading wrapped in quotes, which reads as the app's own words about the
reader. A bubble already says somebody said it.

**Not the introduction, not the rule, not the four builder steps.** Those are
the app teaching, and the reader building their own line. A bubble on either
would put words in the wrong mouth.

**Her reactions are off here, and that is the same call the lesson made.** The
two answer cards sit directly under her, and a thumb reaching past her for one
must not set her jumping mid-question.

**She is the one safe character in the app and she is now reading out "You
always keep me waiting."** The brief rejected making her the person pushing
back, on the grounds that being refused is the wrong job for her. Reading a
bad example aloud is not the same as meaning it, and the introduction labels
the six as examples before she says the first. If it ever starts to feel like
she is having a go at the reader, the fix is a second face for the examples,
not a smaller bubble.

## What the reader sees

> **What happens when you say "you"**
>
> They stop hearing what happened. They start defending who they are.
>
> Nothing you say after that gets in.

> **Say the second one out loud.**

> "You always keep me waiting."
>
> **"I've been sitting here an hour. I'd like to know if you're running
> late."**

> "You never tell me anything."
>
> **"I didn't know about that. I'd like to be told next time."**

> "You're being unfair."
>
> **"That doesn't seem fair to me. I'd like to talk about it."**

> "You made me look stupid."
>
> **"I didn't like how that went. I'd rather you asked me first."**

## Two parts: what happened, and what you'd like

**Superseded 20 September 2026 -- it is four parts.** See "Four parts, not
two" above. The section below is right about *why* the ask matters and wrong
about how many parts there are: it missed step 2, the cost to you, which is
the part that makes a request more than a preference.

A "you" sentence is short because it is a verdict, and a verdict needs no
second half. An "I" sentence has two.

| Part | Does what |
| --- | --- |
| What happened | Puts a fact down. Nothing to argue with |
| What you'd like | Makes it a request. Without it, it is only a complaint |

**The second half is the one people drop.** "I've been sitting here an hour"
on its own describes something. It asks for nothing, so nothing changes, and
next week the same thing happens and the sentence feels like it did not work.

The handout builds both parts into its longest technique: describe the
behaviour, then say what you would prefer in future. These are those two,
without the paperwork.

## The feeling is a third part, and it is optional

**Still true, and it is step 3 of four rather than an extra on the end.** The
handout lists it as a step in its own right. The screen offers it rather than
assuming it, for the reason below.

An early draft said "I'm fed up". That is a claim about the reader and the app
cannot know it -- rule 7, arriving inside a practice screen. Somebody who is
not fed up cannot say the line at all.

So it is offered, never assumed.

> "I've been sitting here an hour. I'm **annoyed**. I'd like to know if
> you're running late."
>
> annoyed / worried / hurt / tired / put out / embarrassed

**Two parts is a finished sentence.** Three is stronger. Neither has failed.

## The one that catches everybody

> "I feel like you're being selfish."
>
> That starts with "I". It still points at them. It does what a "you" sentence
> does.

> **The test is not the first word. It is who the sentence is about.**

## One word to keep out of the ask

"I'd like to be respected with my time" is the right shape and one word too
far. **Respected** says they disrespected you -- a verdict about them, wearing
an "I" on the front.

Say the thing you actually want instead. It is more concrete, and it is harder
to argue with.

| Verdict in the ask | The actual ask |
| --- | --- |
| "I'd like to be respected with my time." | "I'd like to know if you're running late." |
| "I'd like to be treated fairly." | "I'd like the same notice everyone else gets." |
| "I'd like some consideration." | "I'd like a text when plans change." |

## Why this is drilled and not just read

Nobody argues with it. Everybody agrees on the page, then says "you always"
the next time it matters, because that is the sentence that arrives first.

Saying the swapped version out loud four times is the part that sticks.

**The same rule binds the other seventeen.** A line supplies what happened and
what is wanted. The reader supplies anything the app cannot know.

# Drill A -- somebody selling something

Neutral on purpose. Sheet II says to practise first where the feelings are
small, and there is no relationship here to damage.

**This is the light one.** The biggest barrier to a speaking drill is not the
words, it is feeling silly saying them to a phone. Something light lowers
that, and this is the only drill where it is safe.

> **Your line**
> "No thanks, I'm not interested."
>
> Say it out loud now, once.

> **They say:** "It's only two minutes of your time."
>
> **You say:** "No thanks, I'm not interested."

> **They say:** "It's really more like ninety seconds."
>
> **You say:** "No thanks, I'm not interested."

> **They say:** "Could I speak to the homeowner?"
>
> **You say:** "No thanks, I'm not interested."

> **They say:** "Could I call you back on Sunday?"
>
> **You say:** "No thanks, I'm not interested."

> Four times. The same words. Nothing added.
>
> That is the whole thing.

## The joke is the gap, not the caller

They escalate. You do not move. That gap is funny, and it is also exactly the
skill.

**Nothing mocks the person pushing.** The handout's own sentence is *"I won't
allow you to take advantage of me and I won't attack you for being who you
are."* An app that sneers at the caller has taught contempt, which is the
thing being trained out.

So: funny about the situation. Never about a person. Never about the reader.

# Drill B -- a friend, and you want the evening in

Harder, because there is something to lose. It comes after the easy one, never
before.

**This one is straight.** *"Come for just an hour"* is the thing that actually
happens to people. A joke here says it does not matter.

> **Your line**
> "I'd rather not tonight."
>
> Say it out loud now, once.

> **They say:** "Come for just an hour."
>
> **You say:** "I'd rather not tonight."

> **They say:** "You always say that."
>
> **You say:** "I'd love to see you. I'd rather not tonight."

> **They say:** "Alright."

> The warm bit is allowed on the third go. The line after it does not change.

## Three decisions inside it

**The warm prefix is in the handout.** Its own example does the same -- the
third reply is "I am your friend, but I can't lend you any money." Holding one
line for three rounds with nothing warm in it reads as cold, and a technique
people feel cold using is one they stop using.

**The handout's "but" is not copied.** It warns about "but" on the same page
that it uses one. A full stop does the same job and takes nothing back, so the
line is "I'd love to see you. I'd rather not tonight."

**The last push is not a win.** "Alright" is somebody backing off, not
somebody pleased. Ending on agreement would teach that holding a line makes
people happy, and often it does not.

# The nineteen sentences

Two written. Seventeen to go, in the same shape.

## Right 13 -- you can say no

> **You can say no, and leave it at that.**
>
> **The rule you were given**
> Always fit in with people. If you do not, they will not be there later.
>
> **What you say**
> "I'd rather not."
>
> **What you leave off**
> The reason. "I'd rather not, because I'm tired" hands them something to
> argue with. Most people will. *"You could come for just an hour."*
>
> **Somewhere easy to start**
> A caller selling something. A sign-up in the street. Somebody you will never
> see again.

Other sentences that do the same job: "No thanks." / "I can't make it." /
"Not this time."

## Right 10 -- you can say it hurt

> **Saying that something hurt is allowed.**
>
> **The rule you were given**
> People do not want to hear that you feel bad, so keep it in.
>
> **What you say**
> "That hurt."
>
> **What you leave off**
> Two things. The bit that takes it back -- "that hurt, but I know you didn't
> mean it" -- because the second half cancels the first and it is the half
> they hear. And the bit about them. "That hurt" says what happened. "You're
> so thoughtless" says what they are, and nothing after it gets heard.
>
> **Somewhere easy to start**
> Out loud, on your own, about something that already happened. Nobody has to
> be in the room the first time.

Other sentences that do the same job, all of them on you or on the thing that
happened: "I didn't like that." / "That one stung." / "I feel hurt."

## Where the "leave it off" rule comes from

Sheet II makes the point twice, and it is the most useful thing in it:

- A statement is **brief and specific**. Padding is what makes it soft enough
  to ignore.
- A "but" in the middle **devalues everything before it**. Its example is "I
  appreciate your feelings, but...", which it calls aggression in a polite
  word.

The explanation for line 13 found the same thing from the other side: the
reason is what people argue with.

# The price is said out loud too

Sheet II has a section most training skips.

> Friends and family may have benefited from you being passive, and may
> sabotage your new assertiveness.
>
> You are reshaping beliefs you have held since childhood, and this can be
> frightening.
>
> There is no guarantee of outcome.

**Keep it.** An app that teaches saying no, and never says it can cost you
something, is wrong the first time it costs somebody something. Rule 9 bans
promises the app cannot keep, and "this will go well" is one.

In the house voice it is about three sentences, and it belongs at the start of
the whole thing, not at the end.
