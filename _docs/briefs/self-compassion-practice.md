# Saying it to yourself

The self-compassion half of the practice screens. Assertiveness teaches the
sentence you say to another person. This teaches the sentence you say to
yourself, about yourself, when something has gone wrong.

**Written 20 September 2026. One topic worked all the way through -- "You got
something wrong". The rest are named at the bottom and not written.** The
point of writing one first is to find out whether the shape holds before four
more are poured into it.

# Lightweight is the decision, and it is not a trim

Settled 20 September 2026, on the user's instruction: *"for self compassion
lessons we keep it simple so it is more light weight. we can teach user a
couple of lines they can say to themselves for each topic."*

It is the right call, and the skill's own top test is the reason: **could they
do this tomorrow?** Having the words ready is the skill. Everything else on
the screen is there to stop the words being wrong.

So a topic is **four short lines and three short reasons**, and it fits on one
scroll.

## What the source does instead, and why we are not copying it

Module 5's vehicle is a *Compassionate Thought Diary*: a worksheet of seven
boxes, filled in with a pen, with the strength of the self-critical belief
rated as a percentage before and after.

| Its shape | Why not here |
| --- | --- |
| Seven boxes to fill in | A blank. The skill bans blanks -- an open prompt produces nothing and then a sense of failing |
| Rate the belief 0-100%, twice | A score. Rule 15 holds in lessons: nothing counts. A number that does not move is a failed test |
| Seven questions to work through | The reader is having a bad moment, not an afternoon |

**We keep its content and throw away its form.** Each of its compassionate
questions has an answer that is nearly the same every time. We write that
answer down and hand it over, instead of asking the question and leaving a
box.

| What it asks (Module 5, p4) | The line we hand over |
| --- | --- |
| What you would say to a friend you cared about who felt this way | "People get things wrong. You're not the only one." |
| What the compassionate side of you would say to the self-critical side | "That's a horrible feeling. You're allowed to feel bad about it." |
| What you could do to cope and look after yourself now | "What would actually help right now?" |

# The rule under everything: say "you", not "I"

**This is the opposite of the assertiveness lesson and it is deliberate.**
Write it down or it reads as a contradiction later.

| Who is speaking | To whom | Which word |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The app | the reader | "you" |
| The reader | another person (assertiveness) | **"I"** |
| The reader | themselves (here) | **"you"** |

Three speakers, three jobs. None of them cancels the others.

## Two reasons, and both of them are outside this document

**The source's own compassionate lines are in the second person.** Module 5's
letter exercise (p8) is a letter written *to* yourself, and every prompt in it
is addressed that way -- apologising to the reader for what they are going
through, telling them their feelings are allowed, telling them everyone
struggles and they are not alone in it. Its worked example of a compassionate
voice (p6) does the same.

**And distance is measurable.** Seven studies (Kross et al., 2014) put
first-person self-talk against second-person and own-name self-talk under
stress. The non-first-person groups were less distressed, performed better,
and ruminated less afterwards. The finding is about the pronoun, not the
content.

**The app already holds this rule for its own voice.** The assertiveness brief
writes it as *"Rule 1. Distance lowers how hard a feeling lands."* Same
mechanism, now handed to the reader.

## Which makes the whole lesson one sentence

> Say it the way you'd say it to a friend who'd just done the same thing.

That is the source's own move, turned into an instruction. "You" and "I'm
sorry" both fall out of it without having to be explained.

# Four parts, because the source has four

Module 1 (p2) names four things compassion does, whoever it is aimed at. They
are this lesson's frame, the way the four steps are the assertiveness lesson's
frame.

| Its name | What it means | The line |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Noticing that something painful is actually happening, rather than pushing past it | "That went wrong, and it's bothering you." |
| Normalising | Recognising the pain as a normal human one, not a personal fault, and not a thing you are alone in | "People get things wrong. You're not the only one." |
| Kindness | Meeting the pain with warmth rather than looking away from it | "That's a horrible feeling. You're allowed to feel bad about it." |
| Alleviation | Turning the energy towards easing it -- comfort, a better angle, or doing something about it | "What would actually help right now?" |

## Two is the floor, and it is a finished pair

Four lines read as a routine to get through, and somebody who cannot manage
four says nothing at all. Same failure as the four-step assertiveness frame,
same fix: say the floor out loud.

**The first two are the pair.** Awareness without normalising is just naming
how bad it is. Normalising without awareness is brushing past it. Together
they are a whole move, and the other two are extra warmth on top.

## One line does assume a feeling, and it is allowed to

"That went wrong, and it's bothering you" says how the reader feels, which
rule 7 bans everywhere else -- the assertiveness lesson offers its feeling
word rather than assuming it, for exactly this reason.

**It stands here because being bothered is the entry condition.** Nobody opens
"when you've got something wrong" about a mistake they do not mind. The
assertiveness case is different: somebody rehearsing a request may be annoyed,
worried, or nothing much, and the app cannot tell which.

Worth re-checking on every later topic. A topic where the feeling is *not* the
reason they arrived must offer it, not assume it.

# The failure case: a verdict about you, however kind

Every skill has the one thing that catches nearly everybody. Here it is the
nice verdict, and it is the failure case precisely because it looks like the
right answer.

## It is measured, not a matter of taste

Wood, Perunovic & Lee (2009) had people repeat a warm statement about their
own worth. People with **low** self-esteem came out of it with **worse** mood
and lower state self-esteem than low-self-esteem people who repeated nothing.
People with high self-esteem improved slightly.

So the line that sounds most supportive is the one that hurts the reader who
needed it most. An app that ships "You are enough" has aimed its best line at
the wrong half of its users.

## The test, and it is the assertiveness test pointed inward

> **Does the line describe what happened, or pass a verdict on who you are?**

A test travels to sentences nobody wrote down. A banned-words list does not.

| Verdict about you | What happened |
| --- | --- |
| "You did your best." | "That was a mistake. People make them." |
| "You are enough." | "This is hard, and you're doing it anyway." |
| "You deserve kindness." | "That's a horrible feeling. You're allowed to feel bad about it." |

The right-hand column is sayable by somebody who believes none of the
left-hand column. **That is the whole requirement.** A line that only works
once you already feel better is a line for a day you did not need it.

### The screen asks it the other way round, and that is a fix, not a paraphrase

The test above is the one to argue with here. It was also on the screen, as
*"is the line about what happened, or about you?"*, and it does not survive
contact with the reader: **every one of the four good lines contains the word
"you".** "You're not the only one." "You're allowed to feel bad about it."
A reader running the test down that list gets the wrong answer four times out
of four, and concludes the test is broken. It was.

"About you" is shorthand for *a ruling on your worth*, which is obvious from
inside this document and invisible from outside it. So the screen asks the
requirement directly instead:

> **Could you say it on a day you believe nothing good about yourself?**

Same test, no shorthand, and it is the sentence the requirement was already
written as. It also names the day the reader is actually having.

## Watch the rule reappearing inside the model lines

Read every line back against the test. "You're being too hard on yourself" is
a verdict -- it rules on how the reader is judging, and it argues with them.
"That's a harsh thing to say to yourself" describes the sentence instead. Same
warmth, and only one of them can be said by somebody who disagrees.

## One word to keep out: "but"

"That was hard, **but** other people have it worse."

The second half deletes the first, and the second half is the one that stays.
A full stop does the job the "but" was reaching for and takes nothing back.

This is not normalising. Normalising says *others feel this too*. Comparison
says *you have no business feeling it*. They use the same raw material and do
opposite things.

# The reading level is a rule, not a preference

Settled 20 September 2026, on the user's instruction: *"the lessons should feel
very easy to understand."*

This is a teaching screen, so the skill lets it instruct, name its subject and
use "we". **None of that is a licence to be clever.** The reader is learning
something new on a day something went wrong, and a sentence they have to read
twice has already cost more than it gave.

| Keep out | Use instead |
| --- | --- |
| A word that belongs to the brief, not the reader -- "verdict", "normalising", "alleviation" | The plain description: *about you*, *not just you*, *what would help* |
| A clever turn of phrase -- "not a promise about your Tuesday" | The plain one -- "not a promise about your day" |
| A sentence that explains the explanation | Cut it. One reason per point |
| An abstract noun where a concrete one fits | Name the thing that happens |

**"Verdict" is this document's word and the reader never sees it.** It is the
right word for arguing about the design and the wrong one on a phone. The test
carries perfectly without it: *is the line about what happened, or about you?*
-- and that version can be held in the head, which is the whole job.

**The four-part names are the same case.** Awareness, normalising, kindness
and alleviation are the frame's names in the source and in the table above.
On the screen each one is a plain instruction: say what happened, say it's not
just you, say the kind bit, ask what would help.

**The worked test: read it out to somebody who did not ask for a lesson.**
Anywhere they would say "what does that mean?", the sentence is wrong -- not
the reader.

# Topic 1 -- You got something wrong

**Build this one.** It is the most common trigger in Module 1's own screening
list, and it is where the verdict test is easiest to see.

## What the page says

> **Being kinder to yourself**
>
> Most people are much harder on themselves than they'd ever be on a friend.
> If a friend had done it, you'd know what to say. Saying it to yourself is
> oddly difficult.
>
> It's a skill, not a personality. Nobody gets taught it, and it takes a bit of
> practice. People who get the hang of it tend to have less anxiety and low
> mood to deal with -- that's true across a lot of people, not a promise about
> your day.

> **Today: when you've got something wrong**
>
> Here's the sentence that usually turns up first. *I'm such an idiot. I always
> do this.*
>
> That's a harsh thing to say to somebody. Your body doesn't make an exception
> when the somebody is you -- it tenses up and braces, on top of whatever has
> already gone wrong.
>
> And it doesn't give you anything to do -- it just passes judgement on you.
>
> Here are four lines to try instead.

> **The four lines**
>
> | | |
> | --- | --- |
> | Say what happened | *"That went wrong, and it's bothering you."* |
> | Say it's not just you | *"People get things wrong. You're not the only one."* |
> | Say the kind bit | *"That's a horrible feeling. You're allowed to feel bad about it."* |
> | Ask what would help | *"What would actually help right now?"* |
>
> Use these whenever you catch that critical voice starting up. Say them the
> way you'd say them to a friend.
>
> You don't need all four. The first two on their own are enough.

> **Your turn**
>
> Say the first two out loud -- actually out loud, not in your head. It feels
> silly the first time. That's normal.

> **The one that catches everybody**
>
> *"You did your best. That's all that matters."*
>
> Kind, and it's the one most people reach for.
>
> The four lines don't ask you to believe anything. You can say them while you
> still feel terrible.
>
> **The test: could you say it on a day you believe nothing good about
> yourself?**

> **Before you try it on a bad day**
>
> Start on something small. A mistake from last week, not the one you're
> dreading.
>
> The first go probably won't feel like much. That isn't you being bad at this.
> It does get easier.

**Every line above is written for this app.** Where a thought is the source's,
the page it came from is named in the sections above, and the sentence is not.

## Why each of those is there

| Section | Doing what | Would break if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Being kinder to yourself | Names the subject, says it is learnable, hedges the benefit | It opened on the lines. A rule with no name and nothing to recognise next time |
| Today: when you've got something wrong | The mechanism, in the reader's own worst sentence. Two costs, in this order: it hurts, and it is no use | It described self-compassion in general. Nothing to do tomorrow |
| The four lines | Model. One event, four parts, read straight down | The four lines were four different situations. Four things to hold instead of one |
| Your turn | Rehearsal, said once, plainly | It hedged. "You might like to try saying..." is the house voice leaking in |
| The one that catches everybody | The failure case, with a portable test | It listed banned phrases. A list does not travel |
| Before you try it on a bad day | Start easy, expect a flat first go | It was dropped, which is what lessons do. Then somebody practises on the worst thing they have and files the skill under things that do not work |

## The tone line is the source's idea, and it is cut -- reopen it if the lesson ever feels thin

Module 5 (p3) makes the point that it is not only the content of self-critical
talk that hurts but the voice it arrives in -- cold, harsh, attacking -- and
its diary gives the tone a box of its own.

The words can be perfect and delivered in the same flat bark the
self-criticism used, in which case nothing has changed. It is the exact twin
of the assertiveness lesson's advice about keeping the voice calm and the pace
even, and it gets cut for the same reason: next to the words, it looks like
padding.

# Settled

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| How long is a topic? | Four lines, three short reasons, one scroll |
| "I" or "you" in the lines? | **"you"**. The source's letter, and the distancing evidence |
| How many lines? | Four, with two as a finished pair |
| Any scores or ratings? | No. The source rates twice and we drop both |
| Any blanks for the reader to fill? | No. Every line is supplied |
| Can it promise anything? | Only "it does get easier". The benefit is hedged as a pattern across people |
| Does it name its subject? | Yes. A lesson names what it teaches |
| How hard may a sentence be? | Readable once, at speed, on a bad day. "Verdict" and the four part-names stay in this file |
| Is the thought diary built? | No. Its content is mined, its form is rejected |
| Are the source PDFs in the repo? | No. `.gitignore`d, read locally, personal use only |

# Open

- **Where it lives.** No route, no tab slot. Same open question the
  assertiveness brief has, and the two should be answered together -- they are
  one shelf, not two.
- **What is on the screen.** The assertiveness brief reserves cloud, fire and
  wave for these lessons, *"where the feeling is the topic"*. Not decided here.
  The assertiveness screen settled on one idle pose and the layout doing the
  teaching; until a topic is on a phone, that is the thing to copy rather than
  improve on.
- **The fear of self-compassion.** Module 1 is blunt that some people find the
  whole idea off-putting, or expect it to make them lazy and self-indulgent,
  and that a few find it genuinely frightening -- for whom it recommends
  working with a professional rather than alone. Module 2 is the whole of that
  and is not read yet. **A lesson that teaches this without naming the barrier
  will lose the readers who hold it**, and the honest version of the safety
  note is a real decision, not boilerplate.
- **Four more topics**, in the same shape, once this one has been looked at:
  you're behind and not doing enough / you think somebody has gone off you /
  you don't like how you look / you're struggling and think you shouldn't be.
  All four are lifted from Module 1's screening list -- the ideas, not the
  wording.
- **Two older files quote the handouts heavily and are in git history.**
  `_docs/briefs/17 UNHELPFUL THINKING STYLES.md` and
  `_docs/briefs/ASSERTIVENESS SKILLS TRAINING l.md` were committed, then moved
  out of `briefs/`. Deleting them now does not remove them from history. Not
  this brief's problem to fix, but it is the same problem.
