# Writing a meditation script

The reader has closed their eyes and handed you their attention. Every line
either keeps them in the room or sends them somewhere else. That is the whole
job.

Worked example: `_docs/briefs/mountain-meditation.md`. Most rules below were
found by writing it and being told what felt wrong.

---

## The one test

Read the line and ask: **does this keep them here, or does it set them a task?**

A task is anything the reader has to go and do in their head — remember,
choose, judge, picture from scratch, work out what you meant. Every task is a
trip out of the present moment, which is the one place the script exists to
hold them.

This test catches more bad lines than every specific rule below it. Use it
first.

---

## 1. Keep them in the room

### Never set a recall task

Choosing from memory is time travel. It is leaving the present to rummage in
the past, which is the opposite of the next eight minutes.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| Any mountain. One you have stood on, one you have driven past, one you have only seen in a photograph. | *(cut it -- "let a mountain come to mind" already did the work)* |

### Never comment on how they are doing

A line about the reader's performance makes them step outside and look at
themselves. Even a kind one. **Especially** a kind one, because it implies
there was something to fail.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| If you cannot see it clearly, that is fine. A sense of it is enough. | *(cut it, and fix the wording that made it necessary -- see below)* |

When a reassurance feels needed, that is a signal the instruction above it is
too demanding. Fix the instruction, do not bolt on comfort.

The mountain script's original instruction was "**see** the peak". For the
few percent of people who picture nothing at all, that is a failure on line
one, hence the reassurance. The fix was to stop asking for a picture: "let a
mountain come to mind", then "The peak. The sides. The wide base." Nothing
asks to see, so nothing can be missing, and the reassurance is not needed.

### Never name the thing you mean to rule out

To understand "not dead" the reader has to build *dead* and then cross it
out. The building is the part that sticks. It is the pink elephant problem,
and it is a measured effect, not a preference.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| Still, but not dead. Alive, and going nowhere. | A mountain that breathes, and stays. |

Say what is there. Scan every draft for `not`, `never`, `don't`, `no longer`
and check each one is ruling out a **task** ("you do not have to make it
leave" is fine, it removes work) rather than planting an **image**.

### Nothing vague

If the reader cannot form it instantly, it is a puzzle, and solving a puzzle
is leaving the room.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| Your head is the peak. Quiet, high up, with a long view. | Your head is the peak. Quiet, high up, with nothing above it but sky. |

"A long view" is a concept. "Nothing above it but sky" is a thing you can be
inside in under a second.

## 2. The words themselves

### Check the second meaning

A word with two meanings makes the reader pick one, and picking is a task.

| Wrong | Why | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the long slopes running down | A slope is also a ski run and a path | the sides, falling away |
| the rock | A rock is a thing you can pick up | bedrock, set deep in the ground |

Read every noun twice and ask what else it could be.

### Check the verb too

A verb that is nearly right is worse than a plain one, because the reader
stops on it. Both of these were caught by a reader saying only "that sounds
odd", which is the whole signal you get.

| Wrong | Why | Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The trees drink what the rain left | Drinking is a mouth. Trees do not have one. | The rain is in the ground now, and the trees are growing |
| Birds are moving over the trees | Moving is what everything does. It says nothing. | Birds are back in the trees |

Reach for the plainest verb that is exactly true. Never the poetic one.

### Nothing that starts a count

A number, or anything implying one, turns a settled image into arithmetic.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| It has been through more seasons than anyone could count. | Seasons have come and gone over it. |

"Than anyone could count" is meant to say *many*. What it actually does is
hand the reader a counting problem and a mountain they must now age. Say the
thing happened. Never quantify it, not even by denying the quantity.

### Direct, but nothing is demanded

Pure invitational writing -- "allowing yourself to perhaps begin to notice
whatever may be arising" -- is a known failure mode, and MBSR participants
have said in research that it reads as too soft to follow. Stacked hedges
also make the reader parse grammar, which is a task.

The landing spot is a **plain instruction with nothing riding on obeying it**:

> Watch one arrive. / Watch it move across. / Watch it thin out, and go.

Short and direct. But nothing checks, nothing follows, and no line ever says
what it means if they did not manage it. Permission comes from the script
never looking back, not from hedging every verb.

### One idea per line

A line is one spoken breath. Two instructions in one sentence means the
reader is still doing the first when the second arrives, and they have to
choose. Choosing is a task.

### Repeat on purpose

Reaching for a synonym so a word is not used twice is writing for a reader
with their eyes open. Repetition is settling. `Watch / Watch / Watch` is
better than `Watch / Observe / Notice`.

### Cut every word that does no work

"Let it fill out slowly" was cut from the mountain script and nothing was
lost. A held pause does what that sentence was claiming to do, and does it
better, because silence is not a thing to process.

## 3. Body sensations

### Offer the words, do not demand them

Research on MBSR classes is consistent: people find it genuinely hard to put
words to what their body is doing. They report emotions and judgements
instead. An open question -- "what do you feel in your body?" -- produces a
blank and then a sense of failing.

Offer a short menu and keep the door open:

> Maybe something tight. Maybe something sad. Maybe something you cannot
> name. / Maybe nothing much at all. That is weather too.

Three or four options, then an explicit "or nothing". The last one matters
most: without it, finding nothing reads as a wrong answer.

### Observe, never remove

This is the rule most easily broken by accident. Telling somebody to make a
feeling go away is suppression, and suppression reliably makes feelings
stronger and longer. Every line about a feeling must be an instruction to
**look**, never to **push**.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| Let it go. Breathe it out. Release the tension. | Let it be there. The mountain does not fix the weather. |

"Let go" is the trap word. It sounds permissive and means *get rid of*.

### Give them a way out, and say so

Trauma-informed practice calls these **choice points** -- named moments where
the reader can change what they are doing without leaving and without
failing. They matter because sustained attention on the body can destabilise
somebody with trauma or active dissociation, and the thing that protects
against it is the reader keeping control.

Every script that turns attention inward needs one line that hands control
back.

**Make it a standing permission, not a fork, and give it at the very start.**
This is the rule that is easiest to get wrong, and the wrong version looks
more caring than the right one.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| *(at the turn inward)* If you would rather stay with the mountain and the sky, stay there. | *(in the settling, line five)* You can stop whenever you want. Nothing here has to be finished. |

The wrong version was written to put the way out exactly where it would be
needed. Two things are wrong with it:

- **It is a decision.** It stops somebody four minutes deep and makes them
  weigh two options. Deciding is work, and removing work is the entire job.
  A choice point that costs a choice is not a kindness.
- **It predicts distress.** Offering an escape right before the hard part
  tells the reader the hard part is coming. That plants what it meant to
  cushion -- the same failure as naming what you mean to rule out.

A permission has neither problem. It needs no answer, costs nothing to hear,
and is still true five minutes later. Say it early, while the reader is still
surfaced and a sentence is cheap, and let it cover the whole session.

Phrase it about the **session**, never about the reader. "You can stop
whenever you want" is a fact about the script. "If it gets too much for you,
stop" is a prediction about them.

### Never say "deep breath"

App-wide rule. A stretched in-breath drops carbon dioxide and produces
breathlessness, dizziness and tingling -- the exact sensations these scripts
exist to settle. Someone anxious can open any tab.
`_docs/affirmation-flow.md` holds the evidence. Also banned: "breathe deeply",
"fill your lungs", "big breath in".

### Do not park attention on one spot for long

During high arousal, holding attention on the chest or the throat feeds the
loop -- notice the thump, read it as danger, produce more adrenaline. Keep
inward attention moving, or keep it wide.

## 4. Claims the script may not make

### Never score the session

"Well done", "you did it", "notice how much calmer you feel" are all marks
out of ten, and somebody who feels worse has then failed a test they did not
sign up for. State what happened as fact and stop.

### Never turn their suffering into a lesson

Imagery about growth after hardship is welcome as **scenery**. It becomes a
claim about the reader the moment one line connects it to them.

| Fine | Never |
| --- | --- |
| The trees drink what the rain left in the ground, and they grow. | And you will grow from this too. |

Nobody gets to tell somebody still inside a bad thing that it is good for
them. Keep the picture in the world and let the reader make the link, or not.

### Never model giving in

Metaphor scripts have a stand-in -- a mountain, a tree, the sky -- and the
reader is told to be it. Everything that stand-in does is therefore advice,
including the things you did not mean as advice.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| It does not argue with the storm. | Through the whole thing, it stays where it is. |

The wrong line is only weather if you read it as geology. Read as advice, it
says do not push back when something bad is happening to you. Accepting a
**feeling** is what these scripts are for. Accepting a **situation** the
reader could change is learned helplessness, and no meditation gets to
recommend it.

The test: take every line the stand-in appears in, and read it as an
instruction to a person in a bad situation. Endurance is fine -- *stays*,
*holds*, *is still there afterwards*. Surrender is not -- *does not argue*,
*gives in*, *stops fighting*, *accepts it*.

### Close soft, and without a job

The last line is what they carry out. It may not be an instruction, a
homework task, or a claim about what they now know.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| You already know what to do with it. | You can let it come and go, the way the mountain does. |

The wrong version asserts the reader learned something and hands them a duty.
The right version offers something and leaves.

## 5. Mechanics

- Second person, present tense, always. "You", never "we" or "one".
- Write for **one** listener, not a room.
- One line per spoken breath. Blank line between lines in the file.
- `[pause Ns]` marks a held silence. It is not a spoken line, and it is not
  decoration -- it is where the work happens. Settling sections take 5-8
  seconds, sections asking the reader to look at something take 10-12.
- **An instruction to watch something happen needs the time for it to
  happen.** Three watching lines in a row with no gaps is a list being read
  out, and the reader ends up watching the words instead. Each one gets its
  own pause, sized to the thing:

  > Watch one arrive. / `[pause 8s]` / Watch it move across. / `[pause 8s]` /
  > Watch it thin out, and go. / `[pause 10s]`

  A cloud crossing the sky cannot take two seconds. The pause is not a rest
  between instructions -- it **is** the instruction.
- Structure the script in **named parts** with a table at the top: part, its
  job, line count, rough timing. If a part's job cannot be written in one
  short sentence, the part is doing two things.
- Estimate at roughly 9 seconds a line including its share of pauses, then
  check by reading aloud with a timer. The timer is the only real test.
- **Length is judged against the room, not against a number.** The panic
  script is ten short lines because working memory is impaired at the peak
  of a panic attack. That reasoning does not travel. A meditation opened by
  a calm person who chose to sit down has no such limit -- ten to fifteen
  minutes is the ordinary length of a guided session, and the mountain
  script sits at ten and a half without straining.

  Do not import the panic rule by habit. What still holds everywhere: no
  line may be filler, and a part that cannot state its job in one sentence
  is doing two things.
- Write the rules into the brief itself, next to the script, with the wrong
  version kept. A rule without its rejected example gets undone by the next
  person, including you in a month.

The **images** are not owned: sit as a mountain, thoughts as clouds, the

sentence yourself, and say in the brief that you did.

## Before handing it over

1. Every line: does it keep them here, or set them a task?
2. Search `not`, `never`, `no` -- each one removes work, none plants an image.
3. Search `see`, `picture`, `imagine` -- can somebody who pictures nothing
   still follow it?
4. Search `let go`, `release`, `deep breath` -- all three are banned.
5. Any line about how the reader is doing -- cut it, and fix what made it
   feel necessary.
6. Every noun -- what is its second meaning?
7. Every line the stand-in appears in -- read as advice to somebody in a bad
   situation, is it endurance or is it surrender?
8. Any line offering the reader a choice -- could it be a permission given
   earlier instead?
9. Last line -- no score, no homework, no claim.
10. Read it aloud with a timer.
